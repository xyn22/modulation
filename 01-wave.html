Lesson 1: What is a wave?

A wave is something that goes up and down, again and again, in a regular pattern. Think of a vibrating guitar string, or the air pressure in front of a speaker. Only two numbers describe this simple wave.

Amplitude is how far the wave goes up (and down) from the middle line. In sound, bigger amplitude means louder.

Frequency is how many full up-and-down cycles happen in one second, measured in hertz (Hz). In sound, higher frequency means a higher pitch.

Period is the time one full cycle takes. It is just 1 ÷ frequency: faster waves have shorter cycles. For example, at 2 Hz there are 2 cycles each second, so each cycle takes half a second (500 ms). The first cycle is highlighted in green on the graph.

The math

1. Radians and the unit circle

Take a point moving around a circle of radius 1. Measure the angle \(\theta\) by the length of arc the point has travelled: that is an angle in radians. The full circumference is \(2\pi r = 2\pi\), so one full turn is \(2\pi\) radians (= 360°), half a turn is \(\pi\), a quarter turn is \(\pi/2\).

The point's coordinates are \((\cos\theta, \sin\theta)\). So \(\sin\theta\) is simply the height of a point going round a circle. Every full turn the height repeats, which is why \[ \sin(\theta + 2\pi) = \sin\theta. \] A sine wave is what you get when you plot that height while the point keeps going round.

2. From angle to time: where \(2\pi f\) comes from

Now let the point go round \(f\) times per second. Each turn is \(2\pi\) radians, so the angle grows by \(2\pi f\) radians every second: \[ \theta(t) = 2\pi f\, t = \omega t, \qquad \omega = 2\pi f. \] \(\omega\) (omega) is the angular frequency in radians per second; \(f\) is the ordinary frequency in turns (cycles) per second. They describe the same speed in different units, like km/h and m/s. Scaling the height by \(A\) gives the wave on this page: \[ y(t) = A \sin(2\pi f t + \varphi). \]

Check the period. One period later, \(t \to t + T\) with \(T = 1/f\): \[ \sin\!\big(2\pi f (t + T) + \varphi\big) = \sin(2\pi f t + \varphi + 2\pi f T) = \sin(2\pi f t + \varphi + 2\pi) = \sin(2\pi f t + \varphi). \] The \(2\pi\) is exactly what makes "one period" equal to "one full turn".

3. Phase, and why sine and cosine are the same wave

The phase \(\varphi\) is the starting angle at \(t = 0\): where on the circle the point starts. Changing it slides the wave sideways in time by \(\varphi / \omega\) seconds.

Cosine is just sine started a quarter turn ahead: \[ \cos\theta = \sin\!\left(\theta + \tfrac{\pi}{2}\right), \qquad \sin\theta = \cos\!\left(\theta - \tfrac{\pi}{2}\right). \] So "sine wave" and "cosine wave" are the same shape with different phases. Signal-processing books usually write signals with \(\cos\), and so will our code: \(x(t) = A\cos(2\pi f t + \varphi)\).

4. A calculus refresher: how fast does a wave change?

Differentiate with the chain rule (the derivative of \(\sin u\) is \(\cos u \cdot u'\)): \[ \frac{d}{dt}\, A\sin(\omega t) = A\omega \cos(\omega t), \qquad \frac{d^2}{dt^2}\, A\sin(\omega t) = -\omega^2 A \sin(\omega t). \]

  • The slope is proportional to \(\omega\): double the frequency and the wave changes twice as fast. That will matter when we build receivers that must follow fast signals.
  • The second line says \(y'' = -\omega^2 y\). That differential equation describes anything that oscillates: a mass on a spring, a pendulum, an LC circuit in a radio. Sinusoids are its solutions, which is why they appear everywhere in nature and in electronics.

Pen-and-paper exercises

  1. A wave has \(f = 50\) Hz. What are \(T\) and \(\omega\)?
  2. Convert 45°, 90° and 270° to radians.
  3. What phase \(\varphi\) makes \(\sin(\omega t + \varphi)\) equal to \(-\sin(\omega t)\)?
  4. What is the maximum slope of \(y(t) = 2\sin(2\pi \cdot 10\, t)\)?
Show answers
  1. \(T = 1/50 = 20\) ms, \(\omega = 2\pi \cdot 50 \approx 314.2\) rad/s.
  2. \(\pi/4\), \(\pi/2\), \(3\pi/2\).
  3. \(\varphi = \pi\) (half a turn): \(\sin(\theta + \pi) = -\sin\theta\).
  4. \(y' = 2 \cdot 2\pi \cdot 10 \cos(\cdot)\), so the maximum slope is \(40\pi \approx 125.7\) units per second.

Code

How a computer stores a wave: samples

A computer cannot store a smooth curve. It stores the wave's value at evenly spaced instants, called samples. If we take \(f_s\) samples per second (the sample rate), the sample times are \[ t_n = \frac{n}{f_s}, \qquad n = 0, 1, 2, \dots \] and the stored signal is the list of numbers \(x[n] = A\cos(2\pi f\, t_n + \varphi)\). Audio commonly uses \(f_s = 48\,000\) samples per second. A whole lesson later covers how fast you must sample; for now, 48 kHz is plenty for any sound you can hear.

With NumPy you never loop over samples: build the array of all times, then apply np.cos to the whole array at once.

Show solution 
def tone(freq, duration, fs, amplitude=1.0, phase=0.0):
    t = time_axis(duration, fs)
    return amplitude * np.cos(2 * np.pi * freq * t + phase)
