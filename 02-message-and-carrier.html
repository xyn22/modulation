Lesson 2: A slow wave and a fast wave

Radio always involves two waves: a slow one that holds the information, and a fast one that carries it through the air.

The message (slow wave)

The information you want to send, such as a voice or music. Here it is a single simple wave.

The carrier (fast wave)

A fast, steady wave produced by the radio transmitter. On its own it carries no information: it always looks the same.

Why do we need a carrier at all?

Why not send the voice wave through the air directly? There are two reasons:

  1. Antennas only work well with fast waves. To send a slow wave like a voice, an antenna would have to be many kilometres long. Fast waves can be sent with antennas a few metres long, or even small enough to fit in a phone.
  2. Sharing the air. If every station sent plain voice, all the signals would mix together and nobody could separate them. Instead, each station uses its own carrier frequency. Tuning your radio means choosing which carrier frequency to listen to.

So the plan is: put the slow message onto the fast carrier, send the carrier, and let the receiver take the message back off. Putting the message onto the carrier is called modulation.

Try this
  1. Look at the readout under the sliders. It says how many carrier cycles fit inside one message cycle.
  2. Lower the carrier frequency until it is close to the message frequency. The two waves start to look alike, so the carrier is no longer clearly "fast". Real carriers are thousands to millions of times faster than the message.
  3. Notice that changing one wave does not affect the other. They are still separate. In lesson 3 we will combine them.

The math

1. Two time scales

Write both waves with the notation from lesson 1, using cosines: \[ m(t) = \cos(2\pi f_m t), \qquad c(t) = \cos(2\pi f_c t), \qquad f_c \gg f_m. \] Their periods are \(T_m = 1/f_m\) and \(T_c = 1/f_c\), so the number of carrier cycles inside one message cycle is \[ \frac{T_m}{T_c} = \frac{f_c}{f_m}. \] For a real AM station, \(f_c \approx 1\) MHz and voice is around \(f_m \approx 1\) kHz: about 1000 carrier cycles per message cycle. Over one carrier cycle the message barely moves, so it looks constant. This separation of time scales is what makes AM work, and the lesson-3 envelope only makes sense because of it.

2. Wavelength: the antenna argument in numbers

A radio wave travels at the speed of light \(c \approx 3 \times 10^8\) m/s. In one period \(T\) it travels one wavelength: \[ \lambda = c\,T = \frac{c}{f}. \] A simple, efficient antenna is about a quarter of a wavelength long, \(\lambda / 4\). Plugging in numbers:

Signal\(f\)\(\lambda\)Antenna \(\lambda/4\)
Voice1 kHz300 km75 km
AM radio1 MHz300 m75 m
FM radio100 MHz3 m75 cm
Wi-Fi2.4 GHz12.5 cm3.1 cm

A 75 km antenna is impossible, which is exactly why voice must ride on a fast carrier. It also explains why AM broadcast masts are tall towers, while a phone's antennas hide inside the case.

3. Sharing the air: a preview

Two stations on different carriers, \(\cos(2\pi f_1 t)\) and \(\cos(2\pi f_2 t)\), simply add up in the air. Why can a receiver separate them again? The key fact (proved with calculus in a later Fourier lesson) is that sinusoids of different frequencies are orthogonal over a long time: \[ \frac{1}{T}\int_0^{T} \cos(2\pi f_1 t)\cos(2\pi f_2 t)\,dt \;\longrightarrow\; 0 \quad (f_1 \ne f_2,\; T \to \infty). \] Multiplying by one frequency and averaging "picks out" that frequency and rejects the others. Tuning a radio is built on this idea.

Pen-and-paper exercises

  1. An aircraft radio uses 120 MHz. What is its wavelength, and how long is a quarter-wave antenna?
  2. A carrier at 600 kHz carries a 3 kHz tone. How many carrier cycles fit in one message cycle?
  3. Compute \(\frac{1}{T}\int_0^{T}\cos^2(2\pi f t)\,dt\) for \(T = 1/f\). (Hint: \(\cos^2 x = \tfrac12(1 + \cos 2x)\).) Compare with the preview above: what happens when \(f_1 = f_2\)?
Show answers
  1. \(\lambda = 3\times10^8 / 1.2\times10^8 = 2.5\) m; antenna \(\approx 62.5\) cm.
  2. \(600\,000 / 3\,000 = 200\).
  3. \(\frac{1}{T}\int_0^T \tfrac12\,dt + \frac{1}{T}\int_0^T \tfrac12\cos(4\pi f t)\,dt = \tfrac12 + 0 = \tfrac12\). The second integral covers two full cycles, so it is zero. With equal frequencies the average is \(\tfrac12\), not 0: the same frequency "matches" itself. That contrast is exactly how a receiver tells frequencies apart.

Show solution 
def wavelength(freq):
    return SPEED_OF_LIGHT / freq


def quarter_wave_antenna(freq):
    return wavelength(freq) / 4
